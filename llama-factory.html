Fine-tune your first model.
Understand every step.

The LLaMA Factory playbook is the fastest route from "I want the model to behave differently" to a trained result on your Spark. NVIDIA's version lists seven prerequisites — Python, PyTorch, CUDA, VRAM management. This page takes the opposite bet: you have an AI assistant for the mechanical parts, so what you actually need is the mental model — good enough that you could explain to a friend what happened to the model and why it fit on your desk.

01 What fine-tuning actually is #

A base model like Qwen3-4B already speaks — it read most of the internet. Fine-tuning doesn't teach it new facts so much as new habits: how to answer, in what tone, in what format, as whom. You show it a few hundred examples of the behavior you want, and an optimizer nudges its weights until producing your kind of answer becomes its path of least resistance.

The playbook's stock demo makes this concrete in the most literal way possible: its default dataset is ninety question-answer pairs that teach the model a new name. Train for fourteen minutes, ask "who are you?", and it answers with whatever you wrote. Nothing about the world changed — the model's reflexes did. That's fine-tuning, whole and entire; every serious use (your support tone, your codebase's conventions, your product's voice) is the same move with a better dataset.

When fine-tuning is the wrong tool: teaching the model facts that change (prices, docs, inventory). That's retrieval's job — see the RAG playbook in the catalog. Fine-tune for behavior, retrieve for knowledge.

02 LoRA in one picture #

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is the trick that makes fine-tuning cheap. Instead of reprinting the whole book, you freeze it and train a transparent overlay: two skinny matrices whose product gets added to the frozen weights on the fly. The overlay is a fraction of a percent of the model's size, so almost nothing needs gradients or optimizer memory — and your finished product is a small adapter file you can share, stack, or delete without ever touching the base model.

QLoRA is the same overlay with one extra move: compress the frozen book to 4-bit first. Slightly blurrier base, dramatically less memory — this is the variant that puts 70B-class models in reach of one desk.

lora_rank in the config is the overlay's "thickness" — how many columns those skinny matrices have. The default of 8 is thinner than you'd guess and works remarkably well; that surprising fact is the whole reason the technique exists.

03 Why this needs the Spark #

Running a model needs its weights in memory. Training needs the weights plus a gradient for every trainable weight plus the optimizer's bookkeeping plus working activations. That multiplier is the entire story of what fits — and the reason "fine-tuning" and "128 GB" belong in the same sentence.

04 The stack you're standing on #

The old prerequisite list — Python, PyTorch, CUDA, environment setup — is really a description of this tower. You operate the top floor. Everything below it is either preinstalled on DGX OS or installed by one command, and knowing what each layer does is worth more than knowing how to program any of them.

05 Your dataset is the product #

Everything else on this page is machinery you rent. The dataset is the one part that is genuinely yours, because it is the specification: a list of "when asked this, answer exactly like this." You are writing the model's half of its future conversations.
