      02 What the box rewards, and what it punishes #

      One number explains almost every surprise people have with this machine. There is 128 GB of memory — enormous — sitting behind 273 GB/s of bandwidth — modest. Capacity is the moat. Bandwidth is the wall. Everything below follows from that.

      Why prefill looks brilliant and decode feels slow

      Reading a prompt is compute-bound: the GPU chews through many tokens at once and the Blackwell tensor cores have plenty of headroom. Generating a reply is memory-bound: for every single token, the machine must stream the model's weights through that 273 GB/s pipe. A dense 70 B model at FP8 is 70 GB of weights, so the ceiling is about four tokens per second no matter how fast the maths is.

      Which gives you two levers, and the catalog is full of both. Sparse activation — mixture-of-experts models only read a fraction of their weights per token, so a 26 B model with 4 B active behaves like a 4 B model on the pipe while thinking more like a 26 B one. Fewer bytes per weight — NVFP4 halves what FP8 costs to read. Speculative decoding then amortises the reads across several tokens at once. Stack all three and the wall mostly goes away; ignore all three and you will wonder what you bought.

      The 128 GB question

      Napkin arithmetic, shown so you can check it: weights = parameters × bytes-per-parameter, and the decode ceiling = bandwidth ÷ bytes-read-per-token.       05 The dual-Spark annex #

      Two Sparks link over dual QSFP ports at an aggregate 200 Gb/s, and NVIDIA states the pair can hold models up to 405 B parameters at FP4. That is the entire pitch, and it is a capacity pitch: you are buying the ability to hold a bigger model, not to run a given model twice as fast. If you own one Spark, skip this — nothing here improves your single-box life.

      07 The CES 2026 update, scoped honestly #

      The headline from January was that the Spark is up to 2.5× faster than at launch, with no hardware change. That is true, and it is much narrower than it sounds. The 2.5× is one model in one configuration: Qwen-235B under TensorRT-LLM, moved from FP8 to NVFP4 and paired with EAGLE3 speculative decoding.

      Under the same update, Qwen3-30B and Stable Diffusion 3.5 Large gained about 1.4×. Mixture-of-experts models under llama.cpp averaged 35%. Fine-tuning gained least of all. So the honest version is: if you convert your weights to NVFP4 and turn on speculative decoding, some workloads roughly double. Which is a real, free, worthwhile gain — and a different sentence from "the Spark got 2.5× faster".

      This matters beyond pedantry: the gains are opt-in. They arrive when you run the NVFP4 quantization and speculative decoding playbooks, not when you update the box.
